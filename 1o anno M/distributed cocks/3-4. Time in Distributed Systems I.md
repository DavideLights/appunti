## 3. 
The algorithms studied so far were designed without assuming that messages arrive within a known amount of time. A perfect point-to-point link can guarantee **eventual delivery** between correct processes, but it cannot tell us *when* delivery will happen. This distinction becomes crucial if an algorithm needs to react to a deadline or interpret the absence of a response.

This lecture introduces stronger assumptions about time and explains why physical clocks, despite appearing to provide an obvious solution, are not perfectly reliable.

## Time Models
A timing model describes what an algorithm may assume about communication delays and the execution of local process steps. The course considers **asynchronous**, **synchronous**, and **eventually synchronous** systems. They do not describe different communication APIs: they describe different restrictions on the executions that an adversary is allowed to produce.

### Asynchronous Systems
In an **asynchronous system**, there is no known upper bound on message delay or on the time between local execution steps. The scheduler may delay an enabled event for an arbitrarily long time, subject to the fairness assumptions of the execution. The model provides no shared global time reference that processes can reliably use to compare events on different machines.

A process can observe the order of its own local events. If it executes $A$ before $B$, it knows that $A$ occurred first locally. However, without additional communication or assumptions, it cannot infer the corresponding order of unrelated events at another process.

Most importantly, if a process is waiting for a response, a long silence does not reveal its cause. The other process might have crashed, its local execution might be delayed, or a message might still be in transit. **No finite waiting time is sufficient, by itself, to distinguish these cases.**

The advantage of the asynchronous model is its generality: algorithms proved correct under such weak assumptions tolerate a large range of real-world timing behaviours. The disadvantage is that some problems become impossible to solve with the required guarantees. The professor mentions deterministic consensus in the presence of even one possible crash as a central example.

### Synchronous Systems
A **synchronous system** makes stronger promises. In particular, communication has a **known delay bound** $\delta$: if a message is sent at time $t$, it must be delivered by
$$
t+\delta.
$$
This does not mean every message takes exactly $\delta$ units of time. Delays may vary, but they cannot exceed the assumed bound. Local computation and process activations are also subject to timing constraints; for simplicity, the course often treats local computation as negligible compared with network communication.

Because these bounds are known, algorithms can use deadlines and organize computation into **rounds**. Clocks can also be synchronized to a limited precision, as we will study later.

These stronger assumptions simplify the design of many distributed algorithms. However, they are valid only in environments where the timing guarantees can actually be maintained. A network that is usually fast is not necessarily synchronous in the formal sense: an unexpected delay may break the bound on which the algorithm relies.

### Eventually Synchronous Systems
An **eventually synchronous system** may initially behave asynchronously. There is, however, some **unknown stabilization time** $T$ after which the system becomes synchronous and **remains synchronous forever**.

Before $T$, synchronous and asynchronous periods may alternate. After $T$, the relevant timing bounds hold permanently. The processes do not know when stabilization will occur; in the version discussed by the professor, they need not know the eventual delay bound in advance either.

![[Pasted image 20260929204333.png]]

![[Pasted image 20260929204553.png]]

![[Pasted image 20260929205221.png]]

The requirement to remain synchronous *forever* may initially seem unrealistic. It is a theoretical condition that makes progress proofs manageable: whatever finite period of stable communication an algorithm needs, it will eventually obtain that period. Saying only that a network is “usually synchronous” would not provide the same guarantee, because its stable intervals might always end just before the algorithm completes.

Importantly, a process cannot reliably tell at a given instant whether the stabilization time has already passed. A delayed reply becomes observable only after the delay has occurred, potentially after the process has already acted on its previous assumptions.

#### Safety and Liveness under Eventual Synchrony
This is where the distinction between **safety** and **liveness**, introduced in the previous lectures, becomes essential.

A **safety property** says that something bad must never happen. For example, mutual exclusion requires that two processes never enter the same critical section simultaneously. If an algorithm guarantees mutual exclusion only while message delays are small, a temporary delay spike could violate the property. A later return to normal conditions cannot erase that violation.

>[!important] For this reason, an algorithm intended for eventual synchrony must preserve **safety even during its asynchronous periods**.

A **liveness property**, instead, requires eventual progress. For instance, a process requesting the critical section should eventually enter it. During a period of unstable communication, the algorithm may have to postpone that progress without violating the property. Once sufficient synchrony is available, it can resume and eventually complete the operation.

### Relationship Between the Three Models
#### Executions
The three models can be compared by looking at the **executions** they permit. A synchronous execution also satisfies the eventual synchrony requirement: stabilization can be considered to occur at the beginning. An eventually synchronous execution is also compatible with the asynchronous model, which imposes no timing bounds.

Thus, for the sets of allowed executions:
$$
\mathrm{Exec}_{\mathrm{Sync}}
\subseteq
\mathrm{Exec}_{\mathrm{EventuallySync}}
\subseteq
\mathrm{Exec}_{\mathrm{Async}}.
$$

![[Pasted image 20260929210718.png]]

#### Problems
The order reverses when considering **which problems can be solved**. An algorithm for an asynchronous system has to handle more possible executions. Under stronger timing assumptions, the adversary has fewer options and more problems may become solvable:
$$
\mathrm{Problems}_{\mathrm{Async}}
\subseteq
\mathrm{Problems}_{\mathrm{EventuallySync}}
\subseteq
\mathrm{Problems}_{\mathrm{Sync}}.
$$

![[Pasted image 20260929210755.png]]

Therefore, an algorithm correct in the asynchronous model also works under stronger timing assumptions. The converse is not generally true. In particular, an eventually synchronous algorithm running in a system that never stabilizes may preserve safety but fail to guarantee liveness.


> [!tip] Executions vs. Solvable Problems
> **More possible executions mean fewer problems can be guaranteed to be solved.**
>
> - **Asynchronous systems** allow the most executions because they make the fewest assumptions. Algorithms must handle more possible behaviors, making some problems impossible to solve.
> - **Synchronous systems** allow fewer executions because timing is bounded. Algorithms can rely on stronger guarantees, making more problems solvable.
>
> **Remember:** An asynchronous algorithm can also work in a synchronous system, but the reverse is not necessarily true.
>
> **Fewer assumptions → More executions → Fewer solvable problems.**


## Why Distributed Systems Need Clocks
Having established the timing assumptions, now we turns to a different question: **how can processes know when events occurred?** Many applications need to compare the order of information received from different sources.

Consider two messages about a lecture: one announces that no class will take place tomorrow, while another asks students to complete an assignment before the next class.
![[Pasted image 20261018164810.png]]
Without knowing which information is more recent, it may be difficult to decide which instruction to follow. Similar issues arise in monitoring, financial operations, and other applications where the temporal order of events matters.


### Time and Indistinguishable Executions
Recall that two different global executions can produce the same local view for a process. With sufficiently synchronized clocks, a sender can attach a timestamp to its message. A receiver may then gain information that was absent from the message alone, potentially distinguishing two executions that previously appeared identical.

![[Pasted image 20260929211729.png]]

For example, knowing that one message was created at 10:00 and another at 12:00 may help establish their relative order even if network delays caused them to arrive differently. This is the intuition behind **physical timestamping**: attach the reading of the sender's clock to an event or message and compare the readings.

However, this works reliably only to the extent that clocks are synchronized accurately enough. 

### Other Uses of Time
Event ordering is not the only motivation. The professor also discusses **authentication**, where timestamps can help reject old messages replayed by an attacker, and **TDMA (Time Division Multiple Access)** in sensor networks, where nodes use assigned time slots to avoid transmitting over a shared medium simultaneously.

### Timestamping Events
A natural first idea is therefore to let every process label its events using its physical clock. If the clocks agree closely enough, comparing these labels provides information about when events occurred. To understand the limitations of this approach, we must first understand what a computer clock measures and why different machines' clocks drift apart.

## Physical Clocks
A computer does not directly observe an abstract, perfectly accurate universal time. It maintains a local time estimate using a **hardware clock**, typically based on an oscillator and a counting mechanism.

The operating system exposes a **software clock** derived from the hardware reading. We can express this relationship as
$$
C_i(t)=\alpha H_i(t)+\beta,
$$
where 
- $H_i(t)$ is the hardware clock of process $p_i$ 
- and $C_i(t)$ is the software clock. 
The parameters have different roles: $\alpha$ changes the **rate** at which the displayed time progresses, whereas $\beta$ changes its **offset**.

For instance, if a hardware clock runs twice as fast as real time, choosing $\alpha=1/2$ compensates for its rate. If a clock consistently shows 10:00 when the reference shows 11:00, an offset correction is needed. Clock synchronization algorithms can act on these parameters to improve agreement between clocks.

### Clock Skew
At the same real instant $t$, two processes may display different times. Their **clock skew** is the absolute difference between their software-clock readings:
$$
\operatorname{Skew}_{i,j}(t)=|C_i(t)-C_j(t)|.
$$

If one machine shows 10:00 and another shows 11:00, their skew is one hour. If the only issue were a fixed offset, processes could exchange their readings and correct it once. But even after such a correction, clocks do not necessarily continue advancing at the same rate.

### Clock Drift Rate
**Drift** concerns how quickly a clock advances relative to real time, rather than its current value. A perfect hardware clock measures one unit of elapsed clock time for every unit of real time:
$$
\frac{dH_i(t)}{dt}=1.
$$

A **fast** clock has a rate greater than $1$; a **slow** clock has a rate below $1$. 
For example, if one hour of real time passes while a clock records two hours, its rate is $2$. This is different from skew: two clocks can agree *now* and nevertheless drift apart afterward.

![[Pasted image 20260929213351.png]]

An initially tempting solution would be to measure each clock's rate and choose a compensating value of $\alpha$. That would work if a clock's rate remained perfectly constant. In reality, oscillator frequency can vary over time, for example with temperature and workload-related temperature changes. A correction that is appropriate now may become inaccurate later.

![[Pasted image 20260929213436.png]]

Consequently, clock synchronization cannot be treated as a permanent one-time adjustment.

### Correct Hardware Clock
Since hardware clocks cannot be assumed perfect, the model instead requires a **bounded drift rate**. A hardware clock is called **correct** if its rate stays within a known tolerance $\rho$ of real time:
$$
1-\rho\leq\frac{dH_i(t)}{dt}\leq1+\rho.
$$

Here $\rho>0$ represents the maximum permitted rate deviation. A more accurate clock has a smaller tolerance; a clock outside the specified bounds is not correct according to this model.

This definition does **not** require a clock to display exactly the correct time or to advance at a perfectly constant rate. It means that, however its speed varies, it never runs faster than $1+\rho$ or slower than $1-\rho$. Thus a correct hardware clock can measure elapsed time with a **bounded error**.

---

## Bounding the Clock Skew
We have just defined a **correct hardware clock** as one whose rate never falls outside the interval 
$[1-\rho,1+\rho]$. This does not mean that it measures every interval perfectly. If $t_1-t_0$ units of real time pass, the interval measured by a correct hardware clock must satisfy
$$
(1-\rho)(t_1-t_0)\leq H_i(t_1)-H_i(t_0)\leq(1+\rho)(t_1-t_0).
$$

The next question is: **how frequently must two clocks be resynchronized to keep their skew below a chosen tolerance?** 
Let $\Delta>0$ be the maximum skew the application accepts. For the moment, imagine a *perfect synchronization primitive* that makes two clocks agree exactly at time $t=0$:
$$
|C_1(0)-C_2(0)|=0.
$$

### Deriving the worst-case skew
The worst case occurs when clock 1 continuously runs at the largest allowed rate, $1+\rho$, while clock 2 runs at the smallest, $1-\rho$. After $\tau$ units of real time, the two clocks have advanced by
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta C_1 &= (1+\rho)\tau,\\
\Delta C_2 &= (1-\rho)\tau.
\end{aligned}
$$

Because they started at the same reading, their difference is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Skew}_{1,2}(\tau)
&\leq (1+\rho)\tau-(1-\rho)\tau\\
&=2\rho\tau.
\end{aligned}
$$

If our requirement is $\operatorname{Skew}_{1,2}(t)<\Delta$, resynchronization must occur before the worst-case skew reaches $\Delta$. Solving $2\rho\tau=\Delta$ gives the critical interval
$$
\boxed{\tau=\frac{\Delta}{2\rho}}.
$$

To guarantee a **strict** skew bound smaller than $\Delta$, choose a resynchronization period **strictly shorter** than $\Delta/(2\rho)$. 

> [!tip] Why synchronization must be repeated
> A larger drift tolerance $\rho$ means clocks may diverge faster; a smaller acceptable skew $\Delta$ means we must keep them closer. **Both require more frequent resynchronization.** Even an ideal synchronization at one instant cannot prevent future drift.

## Correcting Clocks without Moving Backward
Now suppose we do synchronize periodically. We may find that one clock reads 12:00 while another reads 11:00. In principle, we could either advance the slower clock to 12:00 or move the faster one backward to 11:00. ==THIS SECOND OPTION IS DANGEROUS==.

Imagine that a process creates a message and timestamps it **12:00**. A little later, a synchronization procedure moves its software clock back to **11:00**. The process now creates a second message, which receives timestamp **11:00**, even though it was created *after* the first. An observer comparing the timestamps could reverse the order of events **within the same process**. We would lose one of the main reasons for using timestamps in the first place.

### Monotonicity
The intended requirement is that the **software clock never goes backward**, so if
$$
 t'>t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_i(t')>C_i(t).
$$

An application that needs this guarantee should not blindly use a wall-clock reading that can be corrected backward.

When the clock is **ahead**, a synchronization implementation can slow its rate until the target time catches up. When it is **behind**, it can speed up rather than necessarily making one abrupt forward jump. These gradual rate adjustments are often called *slewing*: the clock stays continuous while its rate changes. The course does **not** require us to implement the exact changes to $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in

$$
C_i(t)=\alpha H_i(t)+\beta.
$$
The professor deliberately leaves those low-level details aside and assumes appropriate correction mechanisms when discussing synchronization algorithms.

## UTC, External and Internal Synchronization
Before designing an algorithm, we need to decide **what it means for clocks to be synchronized**. We can distinguish two goals: 
- following a reference outside the system, 
- or keeping the processes' clocks close to one another.

**Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)** is the standard time reference used in this discussion. It is based on highly accurate atomic timekeeping. The professor briefly mentions that UTC also has to remain aligned with astronomical time, but that detail is not required for the algorithms: **for this course, treat UTC as the authoritative external reference**. Machines may reach such a reference indirectly, through time servers or other synchronization services.

### External Synchronization
In **external synchronization**, each local clock $C_i$ is compared to an external reference $S$ (for example a UTC-synchronized server). 
The system is externally synchronized with accuracy bound $D$ if
$$
\boxed{|C_i(t)-S(t)|<D}
$$

for every relevant process $p_i$ and time $t$. The tolerance $D$ is chosen according to the application; it is not necessarily the same as the $\Delta$ used in the earlier drift calculation.

### Internal Synchronization
In **internal synchronization**, what matters is agreement *within the group*: every pair of local clocks must remain close,
$$
\boxed{|C_i(t)-C_j(t)|<D}.
$$

An isolated network may need its machines to coordinate an operation but have no access to UTC. It can still synchronize internally. Conversely, **all machines could agree with one another while all being one hour wrong relative to UTC**.

### Why external synchronization gives a $2D$ internal bound
Suppose two clocks are both within $D$ of $S$. One may be almost $D$ *ahead* of the reference, while the other is almost $D$ *behind*. Their separation may therefore approach $2D$.

Formally, by the triangle inequality,
$$
\begin{aligned}
|C_i(t)-C_j(t)|
&\leq |C_i(t)-S(t)|+|S(t)-C_j(t)|\\
&<D+D=2D.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence **external synchronization within $D$ implies internal synchronization within $2D$**, but **internal synchronization alone does not imply external synchronization**. The distinction between *accuracy* relative to UTC and *agreement* between processes will determine the goal of the next algorithms.

## Synchronization in Complete Graphs
We first consider a setting where the relevant processes or time servers can communicate directly. The next algorithms are
- **Cristian's algorithm** as the external-synchronization approach, 
- **Berkeley's algorithm** as the internal-synchronization approach, 
- and **NTP** as the practical large-scale service built around similar time-estimation ideas.

### Cristian's Algorithm — External Synchronization
Suppose a client $p$ wants to synchronize its clock with a server $S$ whose reading follows UTC. 
The client sends a request asking for the current time, and the server replies with its current timestamp $t_s$.

The obvious first attempt would be to set the client's clock to $t_s$ when the response arrives. That is incorrect, because **the server's timestamp is already old**: while the response travels back, time continues to pass at the server. What the client really wants is the server's time *at the instant the response reaches the client*.

The client cannot directly measure the one-way delay of that reply, since its clock is not yet synchronized with the server. It can, however, measure the **round-trip time (RTT)** using its own clock: the elapsed interval between sending the request and receiving the answer. If the outward and return delays are approximately equal (and processing delay is negligible or accounted for), it estimates the return delay as $RTT/2$ and obtains
$$
\boxed{\widehat{C_p}=t_s+\frac{RTT}{2}}.
$$

![[Pasted image 20261018184924.png]]

For example, if the server reports 15:00:00 and the observed RTT is 400 ms, the estimated server time upon reception is 15:00:00.200. The client uses this estimate to correct its own clock, subject to the monotonicity considerations explained earlier.

#### The central assumption: symmetric delays
The professor stresses a subtle point: **Cristian's algorithm does not fundamentally require a tiny network delay; it relies on the two directions being sufficiently symmetric.** If each direction takes 10 seconds, the RTT is large, but adding half of it still gives the correct age of the reply *in the ideal symmetric model*. By contrast, even a short RTT can produce an error if most of the delay occurs in one direction.

Let the request delay be $d_{out}$ and the return delay be $d_{back}$. Neglecting processing time,
$$
RTT=d_{out}+d_{back},\qquad
\text{actual server time at reception}=t_s+d_{back}.
$$

The algorithm uses $t_s+RTT/2$, so its estimation error is
$$
\left|\frac{RTT}{2}-d_{back}\right|
=\frac{|d_{out}-d_{back}|}{2}.
$$

That expression explains the role of asymmetry directly. The slides also express a **worst-case bound** using a known minimum one-way delay $d_{min}$. Because both directions take at least $d_{min}$,

$$
 d_{min}\leq d_{back}\leq RTT-d_{min}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\text{maximum absolute estimation error}
\leq \frac{RTT}{2}-d_{min}}.
$$

To understand the two extremes, imagine an RTT of 20 ms and a minimum one-way delay of 3 ms. The return trip could be as short as 3 ms or as long as 17 ms. The client estimates 10 ms in either case, so the worst error is 7 ms. This numerical example simply illustrates the course's formula.

> [!tip] Cristian: what determines accuracy?
> The algorithm estimates the return trip with **half the measured RTT**. Its error comes from **unequal one-way delays**. A smaller RTT can reduce the worst-case error bound, but perfect symmetry would make the delay estimate exact even for a comparatively long RTT (under the simplified assumptions).

#### What if the time server crashes?
All clients depend on the server's responses. If that server crashes, they cannot continue synchronizing through it: it is a **single point of failure**. The natural remedy is using several time servers.

### Berkeley's Algorithm — Internal Synchronization
Cristian assumes a reference outside the group. **Berkeley's algorithm** instead tries to make a collection of machines agree with one another, even without UTC access. One machine acts as a **master** and periodically polls the other processes for their clock readings. The master estimates how much each clock is ahead of or behind its own, accounting for communication delay when comparing measurements.

Why not simply force everyone to adopt the master's time? Because the master is not necessarily the most accurate clock. Berkeley uses the **average of accepted differences** as a common target so that the group moves toward a shared value rather than privileging one reading.

Let $\Delta_i$ be the estimated offset of process $p_i$ relative to the master's clock. 
The master has offset $\Delta_m=0$. After filtering out measurements judged abnormal, it computes the mean of the accepted offsets, $\overline{\Delta}$. Each process is then instructed to apply an individual correction:
$$
\boxed{Adj_i=\overline{\Delta}-\Delta_i}.
$$


#### The professor's worked example
The master and three other processes have the following readings and relative offsets:

| Process | Clock reading | Offset from master |
| ------- | ------------- | ------------------ |
| Master  | 03:05         | $0$ min            |
| $p_1$   | 02:55         | $-10$ min          |
| $p_2$   | 03:00         | $-5$ min           |
| $p_3$   | 03:25         | $+20$ min          |

![[Pasted image 20261018191242.png]]

The $+20$ minute value is treated as an **outlier** under the threshold chosen for the example. Using it to compute the target could pull the whole group toward an incorrect clock. Berkeley therefore excludes that offset **from the average**, leaving

$$
\overline{\Delta}=\frac{0-10-5}{3}=-5\text{ min}.
$$

The group target is therefore five minutes behind the master's reading: **03:00** at the comparison instant. Now compute each correction:

| Process | Calculation | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Master | $-5-0$ | $-5$ min |
| $p_1$ | $-5-(-10)$ | $+5$ min |
| $p_2$ | $-5-(-5)$ | $0$ min |
| $p_3$ | $-5-(+20)$ | $-25$ min |

![[Pasted image 20261018191301.png]]

After the *idealized* corrections all four clocks read 03:00. **Excluding $p_3$ from the average does not require abandoning $p_3$**: it may still receive an adjustment, as shown here. In actual implementations, negative corrections may be introduced gradually to avoid moving a clock backward.

#### Faults and the master's role
If a client does not answer, the master may proceed without that reading. If a client's clock is wildly different, its measurement may be excluded from the average. 
Neither rule solves the more difficult case of a **faulty master**: if the master crashes, the group may need to elect another master; if it sends deliberately incorrect adjustments, merely averaging client readings does not ensure correctness. 
The professor points ahead to **leader election** for crash recovery and notes that Byzantine-fault tolerance is beyond this treatment.

The professor also raises an important qualification. Having **one** externally synchronized participant in a Berkeley group does **not automatically guarantee an external synchronization bound for everyone**. 
- If that participant is considered an outlier, its clock may be excluded from the average. 
- Even if it is included, how close the group gets to UTC depends on the other offsets and the procedure. 

Berkeley's basic objective remains **internal** alignment, not a guaranteed UTC accuracy bound.

### Network Time Protocol (NTP)
NTP is the practical time-synchronization service used on large networks. 

Its core idea resembles Cristian's client-server time estimation, but it is organized into levels known as **strata**. 
0. Time-reference devices occupy the level commonly called *stratum 0*; 
1. **stratum 1 servers** connect to those reference devices; 
2. **stratum 2 servers** synchronize with stratum 1, and so on. 

We can describe this structure informally as a hierarchy: it distributes the work rather than forcing every computer to contact one central source.

![[Pasted image 20261018191324.png]]

The hierarchy provides **scalability**, because requests are spread among many servers, and **redundancy**, because a server can use several time sources instead of relying on exactly one. 
As in Cristian's algorithm, each estimate is subject to uncertainties caused by communication delays, and the resulting accuracy depends on the available sources and connections.

NTP clock adjustments also raise the monotonicity issue discussed earlier. 
The professor notes that a wall clock synchronized through NTP **may be adjusted backward**, depending on the system and correction settings. 
If an application specifically needs elapsed-time measurements that do not jump backward, it should use an appropriate monotonic clock, remembering that such a reading is **not itself a UTC timestamp**.

>[!tip] Difference between Wall clock and Monotonic clock
>- Wall clock: is used to know *the exact time* and it can be synchronized with UTP via NTP
>- Monotonic clock: is used to measure *how many time has passed* from an action.

## Why Physical Timestamps Are Not Always Enough
Even after synchronization, a software clock is only an **estimate** of physical time. Suppose a clock is known to be accurate within an error bound $\varepsilon$. If it labels an event with timestamp $c$, the true event time is not necessarily exactly $c$: it could lie anywhere in the interval
$$
[c-\varepsilon,\;c+\varepsilon].
$$

This means every timestamp comes with a range of possible real occurrence times. The professor considers two cases.

### When can timestamps establish an order?
If the two uncertainty intervals **do not overlap**, their physical order can be determined. For timestamps $c_1<c_2$ with the same error bound, a sufficient condition is

$$
 c_1+\varepsilon<c_2-\varepsilon.
$$

Then even the latest possible occurrence of the first event precedes the earliest possible occurrence of the second.

However, if the intervals **overlap**, timestamps alone cannot establish the actual order. Event $e_1$ may receive the smaller timestamp even though, within the allowed clock errors, it actually happened after $e_2$.

>[!tip] Professor's key observation
>The professor's key observation is that the problem becomes especially relevant in **fast systems**: if events are created much more frequently than the available synchronization accuracy, their uncertainty intervals often overlap. For example, a system generating many events per millisecond cannot safely assume that millisecond-accurate physical clocks will distinguish the true order of all those events.

> [!important] Physical timestamps and event order
> A smaller timestamp does **not** always prove that an event happened first. Physical clocks give useful ordering information only when their **uncertainty is small enough relative to the time separating the events**. If uncertainty intervals overlap, the real order may be ambiguous.
